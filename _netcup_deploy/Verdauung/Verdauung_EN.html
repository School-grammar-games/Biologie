Information Text: Digestive System

The digestive system begins in the head region: in the mouth, food is taken in, mechanically broken down by the teeth and mixed with saliva. The salivary glands produce saliva, which makes the bolus slippery and contains salivary amylase. This is where the chemical digestion of starch begins.

After swallowing, the bolus enters the oesophagus. Muscles in its wall move it actively towards the abdomen and finally into the stomach through wave-like contractions called peristalsis.

In the stomach, food is temporarily stored, mixed by the stomach muscles and combined with gastric juice. The hydrochloric acid it contains creates a strongly acidic environment, supports the action of pepsin and can kill many bacteria taken in with food. Pepsin begins the digestion of proteins there.

Below the stomach, additional digestive organs work together with the small intestine. The liver produces bile, which is stored in the gallbladder and released into the first section of the small intestine when needed. Bile does not contain digestive enzymes itself, but its effect makes fat digestion easier.

The pancreas releases pancreatic juice into the duodenum. It contains bicarbonate to neutralise the acidic chyme and several enzymes that continue breaking down carbohydrates, proteins and fats.

In the small intestine, chemical digestion continues and the uptake of nutrient building blocks is prepared. Enzymes from pancreatic juice and the intestinal wall break food down further; the small building blocks produced are absorbed across the greatly enlarged intestinal surface and passed into blood or lymph.

At the transition from the small intestine to the large intestine lies the caecum. The small intestine opens into this first part of the large intestine; the appendix is attached to the caecum. In humans, this region is not essential for survival, but it still has significance: especially the appendix contains lymphatic tissue and can be involved in immune processes in the gut. It is also considered a possible refuge for beneficial gut bacteria.

The indigestible remainder then reaches the large intestine. There, water and minerals are recovered while bacteria of the gut flora use certain indigestible components and thereby influence the composition of the intestinal contents.

Information Text

How Bile Works

Bile is produced in the liver. It passes through fine bile ducts to the gallbladder, where it is stored and concentrated. When fatty food components enter the duodenum, bile is released from the gallbladder. There it mixes with the chyme. You will use the simulation to find out why this matters for fat digestion.

You can also carry out this experiment yourself. Test tubes and the required materials are available at the teacher's desk. Instructions for the experiment are on the worksheet.
